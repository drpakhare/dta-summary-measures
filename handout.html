1. The running example

The session, the labs and this handout use one illustrative review: serum C-reactive protein (CRP) for serious bacterial infection (SBI) in febrile infants aged 90 days or less, with blood, urine or CSF culture as the reference standard. Five studies (A to E) use cut-offs of 20, 40 and 80 mg/L.

The five studies and all their numbers are invented for teaching. They do not describe real studies and must not be used for clinical decisions.

A diagnostic accuracy review question has four parts: the participants (who would be tested, in which setting), the index test, the target condition, and the reference standard (the best available way of deciding who has the target condition).

2. The 2×2 table

The unit of data in a diagnostic accuracy review is a 2×2 table for each study (and for each cut-off). The index test result goes in the rows; the reference standard result goes in the columns.

  • RevMan Web and most R packages (mada, meta, metadta) take the cells in the order TP, FP, FN, TN.
  • Many papers print the table transposed, with the test in the columns. Swapping FP and FN is the commonest extraction error. Always check that TP + FN equals the number with the target condition.
  • Record a zero cell as zero. Software adds 0.5 where it needs to.

4. Predictive values and prevalence

Sensitivity and specificity condition on the truth (read down the columns). Predictive values condition on the test result (read across the rows), so they change with prevalence even when the test does not.

PPV = Sn × p / [Sn × p + (1 − Sp) × (1 − p)]   ·   NPV = Sp × (1 − p) / [Sp × (1 − p) + (1 − Sn) × p]

All rows use Study A's sensitivity 80.5% and specificity 85.3%. The "per 1000 tested" format is the one used in GRADE summary of findings tables for tests.

Sensitivity and specificity are not fixed either. They vary with the spectrum of patients (severity, age, prior testing, setting). Extract these details so that heterogeneity can be explored.

5. Likelihood ratios

Pre-test odds = p / (1 − p)  →  Post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR  →  Post-test probability = odds / (1 + odds)

Study A, pre-test probability 12% (odds 0.137): a positive CRP (LR+ 5.46) gives odds 0.75, a probability of 43%; a negative CRP (LR− 0.23) gives odds 0.031, a probability of 3.0%. These equal the PPV and 1 − NPV, as they must when the pre-test probability is the study's prevalence.

LR+LR−Typical effect on probability
above 10below 0.1Large, often decisive
5 to 100.1 to 0.2Moderate
2 to 50.2 to 0.5Small, sometimes useful
1 to 20.5 to 1Rarely matters

A rough guide, not a rule: the right threshold depends on what a missed diagnosis or a false alarm costs. In a meta-analysis, calculate summary likelihood ratios from the pooled sensitivity and specificity of the bivariate model; do not pool likelihood ratios directly.

6. The diagnostic odds ratio

The DOR is the odds of a positive test in those with the condition divided by the odds of a positive test in those without it. It ranges from 0 to infinity; 1 means the test does not discriminate. It is relatively stable across cut-offs, which makes it useful inside HSROC models and for comparing tests. But a test with sensitivity 95% and specificity 56%, and one with 56% and 95%, both have a DOR of about 24: one is a rule-out test, the other a rule-in test. Always report sensitivity and specificity alongside it.

7. Cut-offs, ROC curves and the threshold effect

  • For a continuous test, each cut-off gives its own 2×2 table. Lowering the cut-off raises sensitivity and lowers specificity.
  • The ROC curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity for every cut-off. The area under the curve (AUC) is the probability that a random person with the condition has a more abnormal result than a random person without it. In the illustrative CRP model, AUC = 0.89.
  • The Youden index picks the cut-off that maximises Sn + Sp − 1 (near 18 mg/L here). It weighs a missed case and a false alarm equally, which is rarely true clinically.
  • Across studies, different cut-offs make sensitivity and specificity negatively correlated: the threshold effect. Pooling them in two separate meta-analyses ignores this. In our five studies, separate pooling gives sensitivity 60% and specificity 88%, a point below the curve the studies follow (which gives about 73% sensitivity at 88% specificity).
  • Use the bivariate random-effects model (Reitsma 2005) for a summary sensitivity and specificity at a common cut-off, or the HSROC model (Rutter and Gatsonis 2001) for a summary ROC curve when cut-offs vary. The two are mathematically equivalent when there are no covariates. Covered in the 4 pm session.

8. Rebuilding a 2×2 table from what a paper reports

You need the number with the target condition (D) and without it (ND) in the analysis, plus two accuracy measures.

The paper givesCalculate
Sn, Sp, D, NDTP = Sn × D; FN = D − TP; TN = Sp × ND; FP = ND − TN
Sn, PPV, D, NTP = Sn × D; FN = D − TP; FP = TP / PPV − TP; TN = (N − D) − FP
Sn, Sp, N and prevalenceD = prevalence × N; then as row 1
Sp, NPV, ND, NTN = Sp × ND; FP = ND − TN; FN = TN / NPV − TN; TP = (N − ND) − FN
LR+, LR−, D, NDSn = (1 − LR−) × LR+ / (LR+ − LR−); Sp = (LR+ − 1) / (LR+ − LR−); then as row 1

Worked example: Study B

"1100 infants, 99 with SBI. At 20 mg/L, sensitivity 70.7% and specificity 85.3%." TP = 0.707 × 99 = 69.99, so 70; FN = 29. TN = 0.853 × 1001 = 853.9, so 854; FP = 147.

Then check: 70/99 = 70.7% and 854/1001 = 85.3%. If the published percentages do not come back, the table is wrong. Record that the table was back-calculated.

If only sensitivity and specificity are given without D and ND, or only an AUC, the table cannot be rebuilt: contact the authors, and report the study as not usable in the review if they do not reply.

9. Decisions to write into your protocol before you extract

SituationSuggested rule
Inconclusive or failed index test resultsExtract the number and, if reported, their reference results. Main analysis as reported; sensitivity analysis counting them as positive and as negative.
Participants without the reference standard (partial verification)Extract the number and their index test results. Never assume they were negative.
Different reference standards for different participants (differential verification)Record which participants got which reference standard; it feeds the QUADAS-3 judgement.
Several cut-offs reportedExtract a 2×2 table for every cut-off. Prespecify which one enters the main analysis (for example the study's prespecified cut-off, or the one closest to a common value).
Unit of analysis (episodes, samples, lesions, eyes)Use one per participant; prefer participant-level data.
Several readers or several versions of the testPrespecify: one reader (for example the most experienced), the consensus, or the average.
Overlapping cohorts or multiple reportsLink reports of the same study and count the participants once.
Two-gate (diagnostic case-control) designsInclude or exclude by protocol; if included, flag for sensitivity analysis, as they overestimate accuracy.

QUADAS-3 (Whiting et al., Ann Intern Med 2026) asks you to draw a flow diagram for each study, identify the accuracy estimates you will use, and assess risk of bias for each estimate rather than for the study as a whole. Its new Analysis domain replaces Flow and Timing. Your extraction form must collect the numbers it needs.

A ready-to-use form is in data/dta-extraction-form.csv (opens in Excel or Google Sheets). Its sections:

Two reviewers extract independently and resolve disagreements; pilot the form on two or three studies first.

11. Common errors checklist

  • FP and FN swapped because the paper printed the table transposed.
  • Column totals do not equal the number with and without the target condition.
  • Back-calculated tables not checked against the published percentages.
  • Cut-off not recorded, or the "best" cut-off chosen from each study.
  • Inconclusive or unverified participants silently dropped or counted as negative.
  • Episodes or lesions counted as if they were patients.
  • The same cohort included twice from two publications.
  • Predictive values pooled across studies with different prevalence.
  • Sensitivity and specificity pooled in two separate univariate meta-analyses.
  • DOR or AUC reported without sensitivity and specificity.
